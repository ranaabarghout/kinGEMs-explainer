Tuning uncertain kinetic parameter predictions in silico

When optimizing an ecGEM built from raw ML predictions, the growth rate is too small. A few enzymes with predicted kcat values that are too low use up the entire protein budget. kinGEMs changes the enzymes that use the most protein budget through information from the ML model uncertainty range. This is done through a simulated annealing process.

Below, a toy pathway of six enzymes simulates this loop. For each enzyme, the red bar is its allowed range, the grey tick is the CPI-Pred starting value, the orange ✕ is the current value, and the lilac underline is its share of the protein budget.

What we show

Precision, then accuracy

We score models on two separate axes. Precision is how far the constraints shrink the space of possible fluxes. Accuracy is how well predictions match experimental data the model was not tuned on.

94×smaller median flux range

We ablated enzyme constraints to E. coli iML1515 one level at a time. Median flux variability fell from 4.7 to 0.05. Adding promiscuous enzymes (one protein that catalyzes many reactions) caused the largest single drop.

The catch is that the fully constrained model grows at only about 3% of the baseline rate. Annealing (L5) restores baseline growth. Flux variability then stays about 2× lower than in the unconstrained GEM.

Bars: median flux variability (log scale). Line: max biomass flux (1/hr). Values marked ≈ were read from Fig 3B. The rest come from the text of our paper.

46reactions vs. ¹³C-MFA

We compared predicted flux ranges with fluxes measured by 13C metabolic flux analysis (MFA). All fluxes were rescaled so that glucose uptake = 100. This way, a model that simply grows slower does not look more precise.

Both enzyme-constrained models are closer to the measured fluxes than the baseline GEM. Every comparison against baseline is significant (Wilcoxon, p < 0.001). The tuned model gives the tightest normalized ranges. The untuned model gives the flux pattern closest to the data.

0.16h⁻¹ onset error (iJO1366)

When E. coli grows fast, it secretes acetate even when oxygen is available. This is overflow metabolism. Standard FBA cannot reproduce it unless an oxygen limit is set manually.

With a finite enzyme budget, fermentation wins at high growth rates because it needs less enzyme. The switch then appears without being imposed. Three different E. coli models, each tuned only to maximum growth, all place the onset close to the measured growth rate.

Gap between the predicted and measured growth rate at which acetate secretion starts (log scale, lower is better). Fig 7D.

0fluxes used in tuning

Rhodotorula toruloides is a yeast that makes oil. Far less kinetic data exists for it than for E. coli. kinGEMs tuned its model only to the measured growth rate. We then compared it with a GECKO model that was curated by hand and has proteome constraints.

The kinGEMs model recovered the NADPH trade-off. Under nitrogen limitation, NADPH use shifts from glutamate dehydrogenase (GDH1, amino acid synthesis) to fatty acid synthase (FAS, lipid synthesis). It also matched routes specific to each carbon source, such as the D-arabinitol bypass on xylose and the glyoxylate shunt on acetate. It did not reproduce two of the reported features.

Share of cytosolic NADPH consumption on glucose (Fig 6A; Rekena et al. reference values from the text).

r ≈ 0.34either way

We compared predicted enzyme amounts with absolute proteomics data (Schmidt et al.). We used 15 growth conditions and 4 kcat sources: CPI-Pred, CatPred, BRENDA, and random draws from a log-normal distribution. Agreement was moderate (Pearson r from 0.27 to 0.46). No kcat source clearly did best. Annealing did not consistently lower RMSE.

Parameters tuned on one condition fit the proteomics of another condition as well as parameters tuned on that same condition (r = 0.335 vs 0.336, across 767 such transfers). Annealing mostly shifts protein among the same abundant enzymes, whichever condition it was tuned on.

93BiGG models, tuned

The pipeline maps gene IDs to protein sequences, predicts kcat values, adds the constraints, and tunes the model. The full pipeline ran on models from the BiGG database, covering Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria, mycobacteria, parasitic protists, fungi and mammalian cell lines.

Quality is not uniform. E. coli and Shigella models stay closest to the growth rate of their unconstrained model (median deviation 8%). The gap grows with evolutionary distance from E. coli and is largest in eukaryotes. This fits where CPI-Pred's training and validation data come from.
